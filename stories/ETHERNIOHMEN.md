**A day of a servant**

He wakes early, before the zone becomes restless.  
He lives at a calculated distance from the nearest bearer — close enough to feel the density, far enough not to be drawn too deep.

The first task is to check the “edge.”  
Not with instruments. With presence.  
He walks on foot through the neighborhood where reality is more elastic. He observes the clocks on buildings, the shadows, the way people speak. If the shadows delay too much or if conversations seem to have strange echoes, he notes mentally. He does not intervene. He only records.

Then he passes by the bearer.  
He does not knock on the door if he feels that the bearer is deep in the state. Sometimes he leaves only a bag of food on the threshold. Other times he sits on the outside chair and drinks coffee in silence, offering a stable presence without asking for anything. When the bearer comes out, the servant is there — not as a disciple, but as a calm witness. He tells him, briefly, what has changed around in the last hours. Then he leaves.

The rest of the day is an alternation between ordinary life and watch.  
He works, speaks with people who know nothing, buys bread. But a part of attention remains always oriented toward the open zones. In the evening he returns, checks again, and only after he is convinced that nothing has destabilized too much does he allow himself to sleep.

It is not a heroic life.  
It is a life of quiet care.  
Of keeping the space safe for those who hold the door.

**Lucid fear**

The fear that does not flee is different from ordinary panic.

It sits.  
It looks.  
It measures.

The person who carries it feels clearly that something fundamental has changed. Feels that the laws are no longer absolute. And precisely for that reason does not turn their back.  
They sit at a safe distance and observe.

They note the patterns: when the density rises, when it falls, what kind of people are attracted, what kind of people are repelled.  
They speak little. They ask even less.  
But they listen to everything.

Their fear is not paralysis. It is a form of sharp attention.  
They know that if they come too close they risk being drawn in. They know that if they leave completely they risk never understanding what is happening. So they remain in the gray zone — close enough to see, far enough not to be taken.

Sometimes at night, when the silence is too great, the fear becomes heavy.  
They ask themselves if one day the door will call them too.  
They ask themselves if, when the moment comes, they will have the strength to say no.  
Or if, on the contrary, they will discover that they have remained so long at the edge that the edge has become their place.

Lucid fear is not cowardice.  
It is a form of respect toward something they do not fully understand, but refuse to ignore.
